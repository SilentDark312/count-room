The Goal

Beat the dealer, not the other players

Every hand at the table is its own contest against the dealer only. You win by drawing cards that total closer to 21 than the dealer's hand, without going over 21 (that's a "bust," and it's an instant loss regardless of what the dealer ends up with).

Card Values

What each card is worth

  • 2 through 10 are worth their face value.
  • J, Q, K are all worth 10.
  • Ace is worth 11 or 1 — whichever keeps your hand alive. A hand counting an ace as 11 is called soft (e.g. A+6 is a "soft 17"); once an ace has to drop to 1 to avoid busting, the hand is "hard."
Your Options

Five moves, every turn

  • Hit — take another card.
  • Stand — keep your total and end your turn.
  • Double Down — double your bet, but you must take exactly one more card and then stand. Only allowed on your first two cards (and, at some table rules, on a hand you just split — see below).
  • Split — if two cards match in rank, split them into separate hands, each with its own bet and its own turn. This app allows resplitting up to 4 hands total — except aces, which get exactly one more card each and then stand.
  • Surrender — give up now and get half your bet back, before taking any other action. Only available on your very first decision, and never after a split.
When does surrender actually help?
Almost never — most hands are better off hit or stood on. The two well-known exceptions, for this app's rules: a hard 16 vs. a dealer 9, 10, or Ace, and a hard 15 vs. a dealer 10. In both, you're expected to lose more than half your bet on average by playing it out, so taking the guaranteed half-loss wins in the long run. The Play tab's hint will call out "Surrender" specifically in those two spots.
The House Rules

The dealer never chooses — the rules do

The dealer plays a fixed script, posted on the table: always hit 16 or less, always stand on 17 or more. The one thing that varies by casino is soft 17 (an ace counted as 11, like A,6) — some tables stand on it, some hit it. Knowing the dealer's exact script in advance is exactly why a "basic strategy" can be worked out mathematically for every hand you're dealt — see the Strategy tab.

Payouts
A natural blackjack (an ace + a 10-value card on your first two) pays 3 to 2. A regular win pays even money. A tie is a push — your bet comes back. If the dealer's up-card is an ace, you'll be offered insurance, a side bet that the dealer has blackjack. Basic strategy says decline it — it's a bad bet for the player in the long run, on its own.
Fewer decks, different rules
Casinos rarely just remove decks and leave everything else alone — a shoe with fewer decks already favors the player, so the table rules usually get stingier to compensate. That's genuinely useful to know at a real table: a tempting single-deck game is a package deal with its rules, not a free upgrade. This app models that pairing — pick a shoe size on the Play tab and both the dealer's script and your doubling options change with it, shown in the strategy chart's caption.
Card Counting

Why counting cards works at all

A deck heavy with tens and aces favors you: more blackjacks, stronger doubles. A deck heavy with small cards favors the dealer, who is forced to keep hitting stiff hands. Since dealt cards don't go back in the shoe until it's reshuffled, the mix left to be dealt keeps shifting — and you can track that shift with simple arithmetic.

The most common system, Hi-Lo, assigns every card a value. Keep a running total as each card is played:

2 – 6
+1
7 – 9
0
10 – A
−1

That's your running count. Because it means something different in a fresh multi-deck shoe than with half a deck left, divide it by the decks remaining to get the true count — the number that actually tells you how rich the remaining cards are. A rising true count is your cue to bet more and, at the more advanced level, deviate from basic strategy on a handful of close decisions. Fewer decks in the shoe means the count swings further per card, so it also means bigger edges, and bigger risk — the Play tab lets you set the shoe from 1 to 8 decks to feel the difference.

Is this legal?
Counting cards is mental math — no device, no marked cards, nothing hidden. It's legal. It's also unwelcome: casinos are private businesses and can bar a player they suspect of it. Practice here first; the Play and Trainer tabs let you build the skill without a bankroll on the line.
Reading The Count

The count says +2 — now what?

The arithmetic is the easy part. The number only matters because of what it tells you: how the cards left in the shoe compare to a fresh deck.

Worked example
Say you're three decks into a 6-deck shoe, so 3 decks remain, and your running count is +6. True count = running count ÷ decks remaining = 6 ÷ 3 = +2. That means the undealt cards are mildly richer in tens and aces than a fresh shoe — a small real edge, not a huge one.

As a rough feel for what a given true count means:

≤ 0
Neutral
+1 to +2
Mild edge
+3 to +4
Good
+5 or more
Great
  • ≤ 0 — the shoe is neutral or worse for you. Bet the table minimum and play plain basic strategy.
  • +1 to +2 — a small edge. Basic strategy still covers almost every hand; maybe nudge your bet up a little.
  • +3 to +4 — a real edge. This is where it's worth raising your bet more, and where the first index plays kick in — insurance becomes correct at exactly +3.
  • +5 and up — rare and strong. Bet big; most of the Illustrious 18 has triggered by now.
A simple bet ramp
Counters size their bets in "units" (say, one unit = your table minimum) and raise the unit count as the true count rises — a common beginner version is 1 unit at ≤+1, 2 at +2, 4 at +3, 6 at +4, 8 at +5 or more. Treat this as one illustration of the idea, not a precise formula — real bet sizing also depends on your bankroll and how much risk you can stomach.

Notice the pattern: the count doesn't just tell you whether to bet more, it tells you when a specific basic-strategy decision is worth breaking. That's exactly what index plays are.

Going Further

Index plays: bending basic strategy to the count

Basic strategy answers "what's correct on average, across every possible shoe?" Once you're tracking a true count, you know the shoe in front of you isn't average anymore — and a handful of close decisions actually flip the other way at specific counts. These count-triggered exceptions are called index plays, and each one has an index number: the true count at which you switch off the basic-strategy play.

The best-known set is the Illustrious 18, compiled by Don Schlesinger in Blackjack Attack — the 18 deviations that account for most of the extra edge a counter gets from playing to the count at all, ranked so you get most of the value by learning just the first few. Most of them work the way you'd expect: at or above the index, switch away from the hit/stand basic play. A few run backwards — like standing on 12 vs. a dealer 6 normally, but switching to hit once the count drops low enough that the deck is stacked with small cards. For those, the rule flips: deviate at or below the index.

Try it
Flip on Index Plays next to the hint toggle on the Play tab, and the suggestion will call out a count play whenever the true count crosses one of these thresholds — see the full table on the Strategy tab.
The real test: doing it yourself
Hints are training wheels — the actual skill is tracking the count in your head while playing real hands, with nothing on screen to check yourself against. Switch to Count Drill mode on the Play tab for that: it hides the count and the index-play hint, and checks your running count against the real one before every deal.
